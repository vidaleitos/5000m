# Durability, intensity domains, and race-readiness without a race

Companion to the sections in SKILL.md on measurement design and on "rep sessions measure
repeatability". This file holds the literature numbers and the construct definitions so the main file
stays operational.

---

## 1. MLSS duration — why the same "threshold" yields different paces

MLSS = the highest intensity at which blood lactate is in steady state. **Time to exhaustion AT MLSS**
(well-trained runners, n=10, VO2max 61.7 mL/kg/min):

| format | MLSS velocity | pace | time to exhaustion |
|---|---|---|---|
| continuous | 14.53 ± 0.93 km/h | **4:07.8/km** | **68 ± 11 min** |
| intermittent 5 min work / 1 min **passive** rest | 15.26 ± 0.97 km/h | **3:55.9/km** | 58 ± 15 min |

Three consequences, all of which matter daily:

1. **A 30-min maximal effort sits ABOVE MLSS.** So `LT2 ≈ 30-min pace + 5–10 s/km` — **but only if the
   30 min was truly maximal.** A submaximal 30-min average is already near the 60-min pace, so converting
   it double-discounts.
2. **Rep pace ≠ continuous pace, and the gap is quantifiable.** At MLSS an intermittent format buys
   roughly **12 s/km**. That is the explanation for "4:07 in 6-min reps felt easy while 4:01 continuously
   was hard" — not a contradiction, a different physiological point. Use ~12 s/km as an order-of-magnitude
   bridge and say it is an estimate.
3. **"60 minutes" is a convention, not a law.** Jones and colleagues argue the arbitrary 1-hour framing
   has no special physiological status; what separates domains is the *behaviour of the response* (does
   the variable reach steady state), which is what the critical-power model encodes. So do not defend LT2
   as "the 60-min pace" — defend it as **the highest steady-state intensity**.

Cited: MLSS/TTE running study (PubMed 24235775); Heck/Beneke on the MLSS definition and 30-min-bout gold
standard; Jones et al., *The maximal metabolic steady state: redefining the 'gold standard'*.

## 2. Four constructs that get conflated

From Meixner et al., *J Appl Physiol* (2025) — *durability, fatigability, repeatability, resilience*:

| construct | definition | how to assess |
|---|---|---|
| **Durability** | magnitude and time-course of deterioration in physiological characteristics during prolonged exercise | prolonged protocol **> 90 min**; monitor drift in HR, economy, threshold |
| **Fatigability** | rate of performance decline within a continuous task, no recovery | within-task decay; fresh vs pre-fatigued |
| **Repeatability** | ability to reproduce high-intensity performance across bouts **with recovery between** | repeated efforts, controlled recovery |
| **Resilience** | ability to resist / regain function under stress (includes environmental and psychological) | perturbation protocols |

**The operational point:** a rep session measures *repeatability*; a long run measures *durability*.
Reporting one as the other is the common error. An athlete can be excellent at repeatability and poor
at durability — and that profile reads as "fit but fades", which is easy to mislabel as a fitness loss.

**Corollary for assessment design:** if the limiter is durability, the measurement must exceed 90 min.
No rep session can substitute, however hard it is.

## 3. What trains durability

The resilience review lists three levers:

1. **Training consistency** and accumulated volume **over years**.
2. **Regular prolonged sessions**, especially containing **bouts at race pace or faster while fatigued**,
   or a **progressive rise in intensity as fatigue develops**.
3. **Resistance training — particularly heavy strength and plyometrics.**

Lever 2 is the design rationale for a long run with a **fixed fast finish**. The finish is not an add-on;
it is the stimulus. Say that when prescribing it, or the athlete will treat the fast finish as optional.

Lever 3 is the only literature-supported reason for accessory work in a 5K runner whose measured weakness
is durability. It is also the reason "drop the gym work because the limiter is aerobic" is wrong.

**What the review does NOT support:** long slow distance alone as a durability intervention. Volume is
necessary, not sufficient — the fatigue-state intensity is the active ingredient.

## 4. "No race" removes peaking — not race-readiness

If the athlete has no race on the calendar, delete: taper, peak, sharpening blocks, and any "save this
quality for later" sequencing. **Do not delete:**

- **Periodic maximal exposure** — a paced maximal 5K every ~6–8 weeks. Its job is **not measurement**.
  It is the only format that maintains (a) the capacity to actually race and (b) tolerance for a
  sustained maximal effort. Reps maintain neither, however fast they are.
- **Pacing skill.** It is trainable, it decays, and for some athletes it is a larger weakness than any
  physiological variable — this one had three consecutive pacing failures (opened too fast twice, then
  too slow) before any of it was addressed as a *skill*.

When prescribing it, state the purpose in this order: **maintenance → pacing skill → calibration**.
Otherwise the athlete hears "another test" and (correctly) resents the fatigue cost.

## 5. The level is observable without a race — decompose it

A single 5K time is a noisy sample (weather, pacing, company, day form). The *level* reads better off
four slow variables:

| variable | why it is stable |
|---|---|
| **LT2 pace** | moves over months, not days; cannot be faked by one good day |
| **durability** (long-run drift) | needs > 90 min to express |
| **5K-pace volume absorbable per week** | structural, not a single output |
| **aerobic efficiency** (pace at a fixed HR) | a long-run curve |

Then:

```
5K performance = f(threshold floor × durability × specific volume × aerobic efficiency)
```

This is the answer when the athlete objects to "you are training an unobservable goal". It also
**localises a regression**: in the case studied, aerobic efficiency had already recovered to its prior
level while the loss sat in **durability + specific volume** — a statement no single race time could have
produced.

## 6. Separate the timelines — they are different projects

| project | what it actually is | realistic order | binding constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Re-attaching a collapsed band** (e.g. 18:40 → ~17:20) | restoring a specific volume that was lost | **6–14 months** | the missing stimulus, once restored, adapts fast |
| **Pushing the sustainable ceiling** (17:20 → ~16:00) | raising LT2 by ~25 s/km | **12–24 months** | **volume capacity** — tendon, bone, recovery |
| **Sub-15** | a different athlete-build | 3–5 years, not guaranteed | volume ceiling + individual response |

Anchors worth quoting (practical, not literature): typical 16-min 5000 m runners carry **90–130 km/week**;
an athlete at 66–85 has headroom that takes **12–24 months** to absorb safely. The slow part of that is
never the engine — it is the load-bearing tissue.

**Use LT2 as the rate indicator.** Measured every 8–12 weeks, it is slow-moving and sensor-light, so it
answers "how fast am I actually moving" rather than "how fast should I be". A flat LT2 across a block
means check volume and recovery *before* speculating about talent.

**Never compress these into one sentence.** "It'll take years" applied to a 14 s/km threshold gap is
wrong by an order of magnitude and the athlete is right to push back. Split the projects, give ranges,
and name the unknown: individual response is revealed by doing it, not by prediction.
