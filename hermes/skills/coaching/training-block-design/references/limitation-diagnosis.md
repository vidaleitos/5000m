# Diagnosing the limiter — without over-claiming it

The recurring failure in this coaching relationship is not a wrong prescription. It is **naming a
cause too early**, then building a programme around the name. The athlete caught it twice in one
session, and both times the error had the same shape: *a single measurement treated as a verdict.*

---

## 1. Start from a mechanism list, not from a suspected cause

When the athlete reports a symptom (here: *shoulders burn and sink after 45–90 minutes of running*),
enumerate candidate mechanisms and give **each one a discriminating self-test with a numeric
criterion**. A test that cannot fail is not a test.

| # | Mechanism | Test | Criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | local strength-endurance deficit | prone W hold | < 20 s |
| H2 | base strength / tissue capacity | dead hang | < 20 s |
| H3 | scapular motor control | wall angel | lower back or elbow/wrist leaves the wall |
| H4 | arm-swing pattern | observe while running | hand crosses midline / shrug / forward swing above sternum |
| H5 | trunk stability | dead bug, 30 s | lower back lifts off the floor |
| H6 | breathing–trunk coupling | supine breathing, 30 s | upper-chest rather than abdominal |
| H7 | gait change under fatigue | side video, start vs end of long run | trunk collapses late |
| H8 | pure duration capacity | any isometric to limit | position correct past 60 s |

**Have the athlete run all of them and report the numbers.** Ten minutes of testing replaces weeks of
guessing, and it is the athlete's own data — which outranks the literature.

**Return the result as a table of verdicts** (confirmed / excluded / still open), not as a story.
Worked outcome for this athlete: H1 87 s (not the deficit it looked like), H2 traction pain (capacity),
H3 clearly limited, H4 positive, **H5/H6/H7 all normal and excluded**. Three exclusions were as
informative as the positives — they removed whole branches of work from the programme.

**A high number can still carry information.** 87 s of prone W hold with *"it would drift down if I
stopped actively controlling it"* is not an endurance finding — it is a **control** finding. Read the
athlete's qualifier, not just the seconds.

---

## 2. A composite test is not a decomposed cause

**The error:** wall angel came back clearly limited, and it was written up as *"thoracic extension
restriction is the main cause."*

**The correction, in the athlete's words:** *"靠墙天使本身并不能精确区分'胸椎活动度''肩关节活动度'
'肩胛运动控制'各自贡献多少。"*

Wall angel loads **at least three** structures at once: thoracic extension, glenohumeral
elevation/external rotation, and scapular upward rotation with posterior tilt. A limitation in it says
only:

> **there is a composite mobility/control restriction; its composition is undetermined.**

**Rule: never name a component of a composite test as the cause.** Phrase it as the composite, then
state what would be needed to decompose it.

Decomposition, if ever needed (only when progress stalls — training for the three overlaps heavily):

| Isolate | Test |
|---|---|
| thoracic extension | seated, hands behind head, extend the upper back with the pelvis fixed and chin tucked |
| shoulder external rotation | supine, upper arm on the floor at 90° abduction, forearm vertical — press the forearm toward the floor |
| shoulder flexion | supine, arms alongside the ears reaching overhead — can they touch the floor? |
| scapular control | standing, arm at 90° flexion; actively protract then retract — symmetric? any shrug? |

**Do not run this routinely.** It costs time and changes nothing, because mobility and control improve
together in the same movements. It is a tool for when progress stalls, not a checklist.

---

## 3. A capacity deficit is not a pathology — and should not be alarmed at

**The error:** the athlete reported pain within a few seconds of hanging from a bar. It was written up
as a **red flag** requiring professional assessment before any loading.

**What the athlete then supplied:** the pain is *only* on hanging; **bilateral and symmetric**; no
injury history; the quality is **traction** ("被自己体重拉着往下坠"), not sharp; and it is absent in
every other position — overhead reaching, hand behind the back, sleeping on that side,
posterior–inferior shoulder stretch.

**That cluster says: the shoulder girdle cannot tolerate bodyweight traction yet.** Passive structures
loaded past their current tolerance for want of muscular support. It is a **capacity finding**, not a
structural one. "A few seconds" is the tell: *fatigue takes time; a few seconds is not fatigue, it is
load intolerance.*

### The criteria that separate the two

| Points to capacity | Points to pathology |
|---|---|
| only under the specific load | present in unrelated positions |
| bilateral / symmetric | unilateral |
| traction, pulling, diffuse | sharp, localised, burning |
| no injury history | trauma history |
| resolves when the load is removed | persists after |
| clears as capacity is built | unchanged or worsening |

### Consequences

- **Do not retract the whole programme.** Find what can be trained *without* provoking it. Here:
  **horizontal pulling needs no overhead position at all**, so rowing started the same day.
- **Do not practise pain.** Repeated maximal hangs are not a treatment. Build traction tolerance with
  a **feet-supported partial hang** — stand on a box, take only 20–30 % of bodyweight, progressively
  remove support. This is the unlock: it makes the target capacity trainable from day one.
- **State when the characterisation would change** (becomes sharp, becomes unilateral, appears outside
  the provocation) — then it is a different problem needing a different response.

---

## 4. Keep the causal chain restrained

**What the evidence supports:** experimental work shows trunk *and* upper-body fatigue degrade running
economy in trained runners; preliminary intervention evidence links postural/core training to 5 k
performance improvement.

**What it does not support** — do not write these sentences:

- ❌ the athlete's shoulder fatigue **necessarily** degrades their 5 k
- ❌ **a specific movement** is the solution
- ❌ symptom improvement **is** competitive improvement

State the direction of the evidence, then state the restraint. Otherwise a symptom-relief programme
gets reported as a performance gain and the real limiter is obscured.

---

## 5. What to verify next when the symptom does not move

If capability improves and the symptom does not, **the main cause is not where you thought** — change
the hypothesis, do not increase the dose. Three cheap unverified items worth closing first:

1. **Upper-trap substitution** — standing scapular retraction, hands on the sides of the neck. Does the
   upper trap fire before the mid/lower trap?
2. **Gripping while running** — are the hands clenched for the whole run? This is the one source of
   *unnecessary tension* that needs no mobility change to fix.
3. **Arm swing from behind** — 30 s of rear-view video makes the lateral component visible in a way a
   side view cannot.

### Do not rush to name the muscle — build the pattern first

The athlete's own instruction, after being offered a specific muscle:

> *"现在关键不是马上判断是哪块肌肉。**需要建立模式。**"*

That restraint is correct, and it comes with a usable mapping — report it as *a pattern to watch*, not
as a diagnosis:

| Where the ache sits | More like | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **medial scapular border** | rhomboids / mid-trapezius | scapular-stabiliser endurance deficit |
| **near the neck** | upper trapezius / levator scapulae | substitution |

Both point at the same programme (Y-T-W, wall angel, W isometric); only the *emphasis* differs —
medial → keep extending hold duration; near the neck → emphasise **lower** trapezius and actively
inhibit the upper trap. So naming it early buys nothing and risks committing to a wrong branch,
while the distinguishing test (the upper-trap test above) is cheap and can wait.

**Log the onset minute.** 2026-10-01: onset at **65–70 minutes**, superior-posterior scapula. That one
number is the primary symptom metric; every 4-week review is measured against it.

---

## 6. Postural endurance is a limiter class, not a nuisance — and it limits the *training* too

The athlete refused the framing that their problem was "shoulders":

> *"我不是一个纯下肢问题运动员。我的限制之一：长时间保持跑姿结构。65–70 分钟以后肩胛区域开始疲劳。*
> *这属于 postural endurance（姿态耐力），不是单纯'肩酸'。"*

Two consequences, and the second is the one that keeps getting missed:

1. **It is a performance limiter in its own right**, not just a long-run annoyance. Per-minute postural
   demand rises with speed (higher arm-swing rate, larger forces), so the same 65–70 minutes of easy
   running corresponds to a much shorter effective duration at 5 k pace. *(How much shorter is not
   known for this athlete — do not invent a number.)*
2. **It gates the training that builds the main limiter.** Critical speed is raised by long runs and
   long threshold sessions; postural endurance decides how long and how well those can be run. It
   therefore sits **upstream** of the aerobic work, which is why it cannot be filed under "accessory".

This is the correct form of the restraint in §4: not *"the shoulder problem costs 5 k performance"*
(unproven), but **"the capacity has a defensible mechanism and an upstream position in the programme"**
— which justifies training it without claiming a conversion that has not been shown.

**Corollary — do not reduce the limb-versus-engine question to a binary.** The athlete rejected
"the muscular end is no longer the limiter, so what remains is purely aerobic" as *too absolute*. The
more honest decomposition is a small set of coupled capacities — aerobic supply · the ability to hold
near-steady-state at higher absolute speed · lactate steady-state — all of which have to rise together
for the next target. Collapsing those into "legs vs heart" makes one of them invisible.
