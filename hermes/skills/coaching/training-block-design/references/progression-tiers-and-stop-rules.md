# Equipment tiers, mechanical-tension limits, and stop-loss rules

Long-horizon supplementary programming. Three questions this file exists to answer:

1. What is the programme under **each** equipment condition?
2. **When** does the no-equipment version stop supplying enough mechanical tension?
3. **When do you stop, switch, or delete** — rather than adding more?

---

## 1. Three tiers, always all three

For every important capacity supply a **full-equipment**, a **limited-equipment** and a
**no-equipment** version. The athlete asked for this explicitly and the shape is right: the programme
must not depend on a purchase that may never happen, and it must not need the top tier today in order
to be honest about where it goes.

**Tier B is built from what actually exists** — for this athlete: 2 kg dumbbells, a 20 lb resistance
band, a table, a door frame, a stable chair, park bars, stairs, a 5–15 kg backpack. **Check the real
list before designing tier B; do not re-ask and do not assume.**

An earlier version of this list missed the **20 lb band**. The next version then **over-corrected** and
credits it with removing two capacity ceilings:

- ~~Rotator cuff external rotation — needs light load only. The band solved it.~~
- ~~Anti-rotation (Pallof press) — the band is the ideal implement. Solved.~~

**Both of those claims are wrong, and the reason is the useful part.**

**"20 lb band" names at least two different objects:**

| Form | Enables | Cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| **long tube band** — handles, or a door anchor | anchored work: Pallof press, face pulls, pulldowns, external rotation with a length/grade progression | — |
| **short loop band** (a "mini band") | pull-apart (**range-limited**), lateral walks, clamshells, monster walk | **any anchored work** — too short to reach working distance from an anchor, and no handles |

This athlete's 20 lb band is a **short mini loop band**: pulling it apart to chest width is already
awkward, and anchoring it is fiddly. **So Pallof press and face pulls are not available**, and those
capacities have to be trained another way.

> **Rule: verify the FORM of the equipment, not its nominal rating.**
> "20 lb" is a resistance number. It says nothing about length, handles, or anchorability — and those
> decide which exercises exist at all. Ask for the shape before designing around it.

**Lesson, stated properly: re-inventory before declaring a ceiling — then verify the form before
declaring that ceiling lifted.** This file got it wrong in both directions in successive versions.

Substitutes for the capacities a loop band cannot cover, with the stimulus dimension each one fills:
`references/substitution-dimension-check.md`.

## 2. Substitution is rarely one-to-one

For each capacity record: primary / limited-equipment / no-equipment / **does the stimulus change** /
**what genuinely cannot be replaced**.

| Capacity | No-equipment status |
|---|---|
| Horizontal push | **Fully covered** — push-up tree (incline → standard → feet elevated → one-arm) |
| Anti-extension | **Fully covered** — dead-bug tree, plank tree |
| Anti-lateral-flexion | **Fully covered** — side-plank tree to dynamic hip drops |
| Pelvic / single-leg control | **Fully covered** — bridge → single-leg bridge → loaded |
| Horizontal pull | **Covered** — a table, a door, a rail are all "equipment" |
| Mobility, arm swing | **Fully covered** |
| Vertical pull | **Partially** — rowing covers the pulling force; **overhead scapular depression and traction tolerance have no substitute** |
| Grip | **Poorly** — grip needs an object to hold under load |
| Rotator cuff | **Degrades badly** unloaded — the cuff needs some load to adapt |

**When a capacity cannot be replaced one-to-one, replace it many-to-one.** Vertical pull ≈ rowing +
prone straight-arm extension + straight-arm pullover + standing scapular-depression isometric + partial
hang. Together roughly 70–80 % — **say that number** rather than implying equivalence.

## 3. The environment rule — check it before declaring any gap

> **A table is a bar. A door handle is a bar. A tree branch is a bar. A park rail is a bar.
> Fixed objects are not equipment; they are the environment.**

Declaring "no zero-equipment solution" for the lats was **a structural gap invented by defining
"equipment" too narrowly.** Order to check: under a table → park/estate bars and low rails (the athlete
runs past them anyway) → door handle + towel → only then the pure-ground substitutes.

**Long-lever note:** a 2 kg dumbbell is not 2 kg of stimulus. With the arms **straight** (lying
straight-arm pullover) the moment arm is the whole arm and the torque far exceeds the number on the
dumbbell. **Judge a load by the lever, not the label.**

**One high-value placement from this:** a **low** park bar used for an inverted row needs no overhead
position at all — arms in front, shoulder mechanics friendly. That makes it the single most useful
provocative-free pulling station when overhead traction is not yet tolerated.

## 4. When no-equipment genuinely runs out

The honest thresholds — name them instead of pretending, and instead of buying early:

| Capacity | Ceiling signal | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal pull | row 15 reps **with 15 kg in a backpack** | real load |
| Horizontal push | feet-elevated push-up 15 reps | weight vest |
| **Vertical pull** | **full hang 60 s** | loading belt / pull-ups |
| **Grip** | hang 60 s **and** 15 kg farmer-carry 60 s | heavier or single-arm |
| Anti-rotation / cuff | the band at its tightest is easy | stronger band |
| **Maximal strength (whole-body compound)** | **not reachable at all** | **adjustable dumbbell or 16–24 kg kettlebell** |

**Say plainly which capacities bodyweight genuinely cannot supply** — otherwise "no equipment needed"
becomes a lie the athlete discovers a year later.

### Maximal strength — state the position precisely (corrected)

An earlier version of this file dismissed maximal strength as *"low marginal value for a 5 k runner"* —
**while citing Blagrove 2018, whose running-economy evidence (RE +2–8 %) was produced by exactly that
method: heavy loads at 80–90 % 1RM.** Citing the evidence and then dismissing the method that generated
it is the error to avoid. It is also self-defeating, because "not necessary" deletes a documented driver
of running economy.

| Scope | Position |
|---|---|
| **This athlete's current shoulder problem** | Not the first priority. The shoulder needs mobility, control, endurance. |
| **Long-term competitive capability** | **It has a place and must not be underrated** — may act through neuromuscular capability, force reserve and running economy. |
| **When it enters** | When base strength becomes the limiting factor. Dose decided by the running calendar — **the winter base period (low dew point, high volume, low intensity) is the window**, because interference is smallest there. |

So the correct sentence is **"not the current priority"** — never **"not necessary"**.

Also: that RE evidence comes mainly from **lower-body** heavy training. "Upper body at threshold X is
near saturating its contribution to 5 k" is a **scoped** claim about the upper body, and must not be
generalised into "strength training has nothing left to give".

### Do not put a calendar on the equipment ceiling

A version of this file said the current kit would "cover 12–18 months". Written, then rejected:
*"不要写死 … 一旦进步很快，可能 6 个月就碰到某条路径的机械张力瓶颈。"*

**The ceiling is a state, not a date** — decided by actual movement quality, load, RPE and rate of
progress, and it can arrive early or never:

- fast progress → a path's mechanical-tension ceiling can arrive in **6 months**
- slow progress → some capacities may still need nothing better for a long time

**Never write "enough for N months".** The answer lives in the 4–8-week review, not on the calendar.
Same species of error as the interval table and the volume target: **a description of how things usually
go, converted into an expectation and handed over as though it were a fact.**

## 5. Tracks, not stages — priorities rotate

**The athlete's correction:** *"不要让②③④看起来像严格的顺序。一个阶段完全可能同时存在多种训练建设，
能力是连续谱，训练是多个并行轨道，优先级不断旋转。"*

An earlier framing presented four things as sequential stages. Correct framing — **four tracks running
in parallel throughout**:

```
Track 1 · limitation management    mobility, scapular control, traction tolerance
Track 2 · base construction        push, pull, shoulder, arm, grip
Track 3 · capability escalation    load, lever, unilateral, ROM, speed, elastic
Track 4 · event integration        arm swing, trunk stability, holding under fatigue
```

- Track 3 is a **property of** track 2, not a time period.
- Track 4 is seeded on day one (standing arm swing), not deferred.
- Track 1 does **not** simply finish — its items either retire or drop to maintenance.
- **Several tracks can be building at once** (winter: track 2 heavy + track 1 daily + track 4 kept).

**Priority rotates on "where is the limit now", not on "which stage are we in".**

Note this is distinct from the **capability chain** in
`references/supplementary-upper-body-core.md` (coordination/position → strength → strength-endurance →
event-specific). That chain describes how to progress **one** capacity; the four tracks describe what
runs **concurrently** across the programme. Both are correct and they do not conflict — do not let one
be presented as the other.

## 6. Interference cost scales with dose — and that is the real scheduling constraint

| Dose | Interference cost | Placement |
|---|---|---|
| mobility, scapular control, standing arm swing (low load, far from failure) | **≈ 0** | daily, hooked to habits |
| accessory strength, 2–3 sets, 3 reps in reserve | **low** | after a run / after the evening double |
| 3–4 sets, 1–2 reps in reserve | **medium** | not within 24 h before a quality session |
| any set taken to failure | **high** | **do not** |

Upper-back failure degrades arm swing for 24–48 h; core failure degrades trunk stability. **One hard
rule: never to failure.**

**Seasonal corollary worth stating:** in the high-heat months running volume is forced down, so the
supplementary block can be **increased without adding to running's load.** That is a free capacity
window, second only to the winter base period.

## 7. Stop-loss rules

The athlete's own requirement, made explicit. Each exists to stop an unproductive trend from
continuing by default.

**Rule 1 — capability up, running performance unchanged.**
Trigger: **12 weeks** of capability-node improvement with no change in matched-HR pace, late-session
mechanics, or a time trial. Action: **cut the accessory work to maintenance** and give the time back to
running. Conclusion: **this was not the limiter.**

**Rule 2 — symptom better, performance unchanged.**
**Record it as symptom improvement. Do not write it up as competitive improvement.** Keep looking for
the real limiter.

**Rule 3 — a movement or a capacity produces nothing for 8 weeks.**
Trigger: no change in **either** capability **or** transfer.

**Do not default to increasing the dose** — the original wording put dose first and the athlete corrected
it: *"不应默认增加剂量，而应重新审查动作、剂量、假设和优先级。"* Review **four things at once**, no
fixed order; whichever is most doubtful, examine first:

1. **Movement** — is it actually training what it claims to train?
2. **Dose** — frequency, sets, proximity to failure, load: enough?
3. **Assumption** — is the reason I believe it helps actually valid?
4. **Priority** — does it deserve the training slot at all?

Then delete if nothing holds up. **Neither "give it more time" nor "add more volume" is a default** —
putting dose first presumes the problem is quantity, which is the laziest and most common assumption.

**Rule 3b — the two questions every 4–8-week review must also ask.**
Not only *"did the movement get stronger?"* — that is almost always yes, because training does that. Also:

> **1. Does this capacity still deserve training resource?**
> **2. Is it addressing the athlete's actual limiter?**

Capability rising while transfer stays flat is the signal for **Rule 1**, not for more dose.

### Rule 3c — escalation has a horizontal axis

Before escalating any movement, ask **whether that capacity is still the limiter.**

- **Yes** → escalate vertically (lever, unilateral, load, duration, ROM, speed).
- **No, already sufficient → move the capacity horizontally.** Do not keep escalating the same movement
  indefinitely.

Move sideways when: capacity sufficient · vertical blocked by anatomy (lever, ROM) · the same stimulus
has flattened · the movement has nothing left to give (wall angel once full range is reached) · one area
has been over-served and another has fallen behind (all pull, no push).

> **Training capacity is a finite resource. How it is allocated matters more than how hard any single
> movement gets.**

A pure "path" drawing — level 0 → 1 → 2 → 3, always upward — implies the athlete climbs forever. That
is wrong, and it is the same error family as a ladder or a lookup table.

**Rule 4 — capability up, symptom unchanged.**
The main cause is elsewhere. **Change the hypothesis** — do not add more of the same. See
`references/limitation-diagnosis.md` §5.

**Every one of these is a *delete* direction, not an *add* direction.** That is deliberate: the failure
mode of supplementary work is growth, not neglect.

## 8. Exercise lifecycle — retirement is not a verdict on the exercise

```
introduce (2–3 wk) → build (4–12 wk) → maintain (long term, ~1/3 dose) → retire
```

**Retirement means the primary function is now covered by a more suitable stimulus** — not that the
exercise was worthless.

Legitimate reasons: covered by a more efficient movement; automated and no longer needs dedicated
practice (wall angel → warm-up); the opportunity cost of its slot exceeds its return.
Illegitimate: "it's too easy", "it's boring".

**Container sizes are the anti-bloat mechanism** — upper body 4–6 movements per session, core 3–4,
limitation-management 3–4. **Adding one means removing one.**

## 9. Verify in four layers, and do not confuse them

1. **Symptom** — at what minute of a long run do the shoulders start to sink? Every 4 weeks.
2. **Capability nodes** — hang seconds, row reps, push-up reps, side-plank time, arm-swing duration.
3. **Running performance** — matched-HR pace, same-pace perception, late-session ground-contact time
   and step length, a standardized time trial. **This is the layer that decides whether any of it
   transferred.**
4. **Mechanics** — late-session drift, as supporting detail.

**Layer 2 rising while layer 3 stays flat is the signal to invoke Rule 1.** Layers 1 and 3 moving
together is the only outcome worth reporting as a result.

### Symptoms and capability are two separate goal systems

State this explicitly, because conflating them is the default error:

| Symptom goal (this problem) | Capability goal (long-term, independent of the symptom) |
|---|---|
| does not collapse · no wasted tension · shoulder-sinking point moves later | base strength · relative strength · strength-endurance · fast force · control · event integration |

**Symptom improvement does not mean the capability goal is met; capability rising does not mean the
symptom disappears.** Pursue both, record them separately, verify them separately.

**Layer 1 alone can only prove that the symptom now arrives later.** It is the primary *symptom* metric
and it is not a *performance* metric. Competitive transfer may only be discussed when layer 1 moves
**together with** running performance, late-session mechanical retention, or a standardized test result.

Corollary — resist writing the goal negatively. "The goal is not strength, it is holding position" makes
progressive overload optional and the programme plateaus invisibly. See
`references/supplementary-upper-body-core.md` §2.
